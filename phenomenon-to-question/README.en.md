# phenomenon-to-question

> From phenomenon to research question — the first link in the chain, and the one least often taught.

## The problem it solves

The first obstacle for a graduate student is not producing results — it is **not knowing what to study**.

They usually arrive with a vague observation:

> "I think everyone is buying 'dupes' now — could that be a research topic?"
> "After our company made weekly reports public, everyone seems to be performing. Is that a phenomenon?"
> "The platform keeps changing its commission rules and sellers are suffering. Is that worth studying?"

And the existing tools all assume **you already have a construct**: the literature tool asks "what is your research topic", the experiment designer asks "what are your core hypotheses". **Nobody is responsible for turning "I noticed something" into "a researchable question".**

This skill fills that step.

---

## Core principles

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Phenomenon before construct, construct before hypothesis** | Skip the first two and what you get is "another test of a conclusion the literature already has" |
| **Stress-testing beats fast construction** | Insight is not explaining a phenomenon quickly; it is building a structure that survives repeated questioning |
| **No assumed discipline** | The same phenomenon may belong to marketing, OB, IS and finance at once. Ask "who studies this", do not label it first |
| **No assumed method** | Quantitative or qualitative follows from the nature of the question, not from student or supervisor preference |
| **"Not worth studying" is a valid output** | When the phenomenon is not real, too small, or unobservable, saying so is more responsible than forcing a topic |

---

## Workflow (five stages)

| Stage | What happens | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **1 Phenomenon anchoring** | Turn "a vague feeling" into "a bounded instance". Demand concretisation; ask three questions: what changed, relative to what, who is affected | A phenomenon statement of ≤150 words (observation only, no explanation) |
| **2 Premise stress test** ★ | Six checks, one by one: reality, scale, novelty, observability, counter-examples, mechanism | A three-way verdict: pass / needs narrowing / does not hold |
| **3 Decomposition and theoretical lens** | Break into components (actor, level, context, time, candidate DV/IV); find theory via a three-step method | 2–3 lenses, each stating what it explains, what it cannot, and **what observable difference it predicts** |
| **4 Candidate questions** | Generate 2–3 candidate research questions; decide quantitative vs qualitative | Each with: data needed, method fit, contribution, main risk |
| **5 Feasibility and handoff** | Five screens: data, time, ethics, capability, risk | A one-page "research question brief" plus an explicit next step |

> **Stage 2 is the one step that must never be skipped** — and the one most often skipped. Students cling to a media buzzword; the six checks force it onto "is there data, is there a counter-example, can it be measured".

## How it stays cross-domain

This skill is not bound to marketing. Three design decisions:

**① The theory section gives a method, not a discipline-specific list.**
"Locate the discipline → find the synonyms → choose a lens", plus a **synonym table of 8 phenomenon cores × 6 disciplines**. The same thing is named differently everywhere: "following others" is *conformity* in marketing, *social influence* in OB, *herding* in finance, *institutional isomorphism* in strategy. **Without the synonym step, students circle inside one literature and miss what three other fields have already done.**

**② Each of the six stress tests carries examples from four fields.**
For "reality": marketing needs average basket size and search indices; OB needs turnover rates and time logs; accounting needs accrual distributions and threshold discontinuities; operations needs import-source concentration.

**③ Eight mechanism families, valid across fields**: cognition / motivation and self / social influence / information and uncertainty / incentives and contracts / structure and institutions / time and process / emotion and experience. When a student says "I don't know what theories exist", locate the family first, then pick the theory — far faster than searching by theory name.

## Example

```
I noticed that after my company made weekly reports public, employees started citing
each other's work inside their reports, and the reports became longer than the actual work.
Is this worth studying? What kind of question could it become?
```

The skill demands concretisation first (which company, what period, which levels), then runs the six checks (is there data? one company or general? an old phenomenon renamed?), then decomposes the phenomenon and offers 2–3 lenses (impression management, information asymmetry, practice theory), and finally returns 2–3 candidate questions with feasibility judgements.

## Boundaries

- **Does not do literature search or gap diagnosis.** That is `literature-gap-synthesizer`'s job. This skill stops at "a question worth studying".
- **Does not review hypotheses or design experiments.** That is `social-science-experiment-designer`'s job. This skill produces candidate questions, not testable hypotheses.
- **Never fabricates literature, theories or scholars.** Only entries it is confident exist; when unsure it says so and gives a verification path.
- **Does not decide for the student.** It gives candidates, reasons and risks; the choice is theirs.
- **Does not guarantee the phenomenon holds.** If any of the six checks fails, it says the direction cannot be done and what would be needed.
