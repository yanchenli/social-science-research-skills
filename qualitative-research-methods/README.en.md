## The problem it solves

The quantitative track has a full toolkit; the qualitative track often has only "my supervisor said
to change it". This skill supplies the whole chain:

method selection → sampling and instrument design → coding and analysis → trustworthiness check → Findings writing.

It is the only **non-statistical** path in the pack, and the only one that treats **quotations** as the core evidence.

**Who it is written for**: business graduate students, and **supervisors who are more at home with
quantitative methods**. That is why the method-selection section does not open with qualitative
jargon. It runs: what shape must the conclusion take → can the data support that shape → is the
method recognised as producing that shape — the same order as "decide what your dependent variable is".

---

## Core principle

**In qualitative research, rigour is not statistical significance — it is trustworthiness.**

Design, collection and analysis are often interleaved — especially in grounded theory's "theoretical
sampling", where emerging analysis determines who you interview next. So the stages here are an
**entry map**, not a mandatory sequence.

> ⚠️ **Quotations are to qualitative work what numbers are to quantitative work.**
> Coding, analysis and Findings must rest on the transcripts or field notes the user supplies:
> **never invent plausible-sounding interview content, and never "polish" a participant's words
> into something prettier.**

The AI handles structure — recommending a method, building instruments, drafting a coding
framework, checking rigour criteria, drafting and diagnosing Findings. The interpretive judgement,
coding decisions and theory building are the researcher's work.
**Subjective involvement is part of the method, not noise to be eliminated.**

## Workflow (five stages)

| Stage | Content | Key output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Method and tradition | Three-step decision; comparison of nine methods; the three schools of grounded theory | Method + version, with reasons |
| 2. Sampling and instruments | Purposive / extreme-case / theoretical / snowball sampling; interview guide, focus groups, observation | Sampling plan + instruments |
| 3. Coding and analysis | Thematic analysis (four approaches), grounded theory coding, Gioia data structure, cross-case analysis | Coding framework + thematic structure |
| 4. Rigour check | Lincoln & Guba's four criteria, adjusted by approach; audit-trail checklist before Findings | Trustworthiness account |
| 5. Findings writing | Per-tradition writing conventions; quotation use; bilingual quotation translation | Findings text |

### Stage 1: choosing a method

**Step one — should this be qualitative at all?** Is the question "how / why / what does it mean",
or "how many / whether / which factor matters more"? Hard test: **if the expected conclusion is a set
of coefficients or a ranking, qualitative methods cannot deliver it.**

**Step two — look at the data you can actually get.** This often decides the method more than the
research question does: a beautifully written question plus eight interviews already rules out
ethnography and case study.

**Step three — compare the candidates.**

| Method | The question it answers, in one line | Common in business schools |
|---|---|---|
| Thematic analysis (TA) | What patterns of shared meaning do people hold around this issue? | High |
| Case study | How does a process or mechanism unfold in this context? | High |
| Grounded theory | No theory exists yet — can one be grown from the data? | Medium |
| Gioia methodology | How do I present inductive work as a data structure reviewers recognise? | High (management) |
| Qualitative content analysis | How much of each category, and which categories exist? | Medium |
| Discourse analysis | How does language construct reality, identity and power? | Low |
| IPA / phenomenology | What is this experience like for this very small number of people? | Low |
| Ethnography | How does a group's culture and informal order work? | Medium |
| Narrative inquiry | How do people use storytelling to build identity and meaning? | Medium |

**A recommendation must carry four things**: reasons, costs, alternatives, and what information is
still missing. A method name on its own is not a recommendation.

**For a supervisor new to qualitative work, three questions suffice**: What will your conclusion look
like? Can the data you have support that shape? Is the method recognised as producing that shape?
If all three pass, the choice will hold up.

See `references/method-selection.md`.

## Thematic analysis: four approaches, pick one first

Thematic analysis is a **family**, not a single method. The four approaches differ not in steps but in
what a theme is and what counts as quality:

| Approach | What a theme is | What quality rests on |
|---|---|---|
| Coding reliability | Fixed early, often lifted from the interview questions; essentially a topic summary | Multiple independent coders + agreement statistics |
| Codebook / framework | Topic summary, but coding is interpretive | Systematic codebook, team coordination |
| **Reflexive** | A pattern of shared meaning organised around a **central concept** | Reflexivity, depth, coherence; **not inter-coder reliability** |
| Thematic coding | Themes developed through grounded-theory procedures | Systematic coding procedure |

**Mixing them is methodological incoherence**, and it is what reviewers catch first. Typical errors:
claiming a reflexive approach while reporting kappa; or claiming reflexive TA while using *member
checking* to verify that an interpretation is accurate.

The skill ships a **congruence matrix** (12 practices × 4 approaches) marking what is compatible, what
needs adapting, and what is incompatible:

| Practice | Under a reflexive approach |
|---|---|
| Member checking (verifying whether a reading is "right") | ✗ incompatible → use member reflections |
| Triangulation (as accuracy checking) | ✗ incompatible → may be used as design-level richness |
| Saturation (as a sample-size rationale) | ✗ incompatible → use "is the data sufficient" + information power |
| Inter-coder reliability / codebook | ✗ incompatible |
| Reflexive journal / audit trail / thick description | ✓ compatible |

It also provides three **topic-summary tests** (could you have predicted it *and* have nothing more to
say; does it map onto an interview question; is the name a topic rather than a claim) and three layers
of quality criteria: the 15-point checklist, the 20-question review tool, and ten common problems.

See `references/thematic-analysis.md` and `references/ta-quality.md`.

## Stage 4: the four trustworthiness criteria

credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability.

The skill checks whether each is **substantively addressed**, not merely named. It also flags the
**approach-dependent adjustments**: under a reflexive approach, credibility uses member reflections
rather than member checking, confirmability is phrased as "owning one's perspective" rather than
"reflexivity statement", and the word "bias" is avoided — it presupposes bias-free knowledge is possible.

**The four criteria are a cross-paradigm language, but they do not judge within-method coherence.**
Both layers are needed.

## Examples

```
I have 14 interview transcripts on how middle managers understand digital transformation.
I used the Gioia methodology and have coded twice. Write the Findings in English.
```

The skill first checks that the tradition and version match the coding procedure, and that sampling and
saturation are accounted for; then reads the Gioia writing conventions; then diagnoses the existing
coding table (are the 2nd-order themes genuinely more abstract than the 1st-order concepts, or just
renamed?); then drafts the Findings in Gioia's narrative structure.

```
My question is how consumers make sense of a certain kind of marketing content.
I can probably recruit about 20 interviews. Which method should I use?
```

The skill runs the three steps: whether this should be qualitative at all; what 20 interviews can and
cannot support; then a comparison of the candidates — returning a recommendation with reasons, costs
and alternatives.

**Missing quotations are never invented.** The skill writes a skeleton with
`[quotation to be supplied: P?, supporting XX]` placeholders.

## Boundaries

- **It does not code for the researcher or decide interpretations.** It may propose a coding framework,
  flag candidate themes, draft and diagnose Findings — but whether a theme holds, and what it means,
  remain the researcher's call.
- **It does not invent quotations.** See the core warning above.
- **It does not treat saturation as a universal criterion.** Theoretical saturation belongs to grounded
  theory and does not hold for reflexive TA (Braun & Clarke, 2021: "often no"). Sample size is judged by
  whether the data sufficiently answer the question; when a number is needed, give a range and decide as
  you go.
- **It does not pretend to be NVivo.** No qualitative software is required — spreadsheets and documents
  are enough, and the skill can annotate, group and draft a coding framework directly on the text you supply.
- **The AI does not replace interpretation, and is not used for verification.** It may generate a first
  pass of codes, cluster candidate themes, and check whether a theme is merely a topic summary — but every
  code must be confirmed or rewritten by the researcher; it must never generate or polish quotations; and
  it must not be used to "check reliability" by re-coding. Disclose which step, what for, and how it was
  reviewed.
