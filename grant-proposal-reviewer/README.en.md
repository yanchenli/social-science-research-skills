# grant-proposal-reviewer

> Grant and project proposal review — judging whether a plan is worth funding.

## The problem it solves

Manuscript review and grant review are often treated as the same task. They are not.

Manuscript review asks whether **completed work stands up**; grant review asks whether **this plan is
worth spending money on**. The latter adds dimensions manuscript review does not have: feasibility,
budget, team qualifications, timeline.

This skill turns that judgement into an auditable procedure and produces two opinions in different registers.

---

## Core principle

**You are reviewing a plan, not completed research.**

> ⚠️ The framework built into the skill is a **generic default**, drawing on the Intellectual Merit /
> Broader Impacts dual-criterion system that NSF has used for nearly thirty years — **it is not any
> single agency's official standard**. If the funder (an internal faculty grant, NSFC, the Macao
> Science and Technology Development Fund, …) publishes its own criteria and weights, **those govern**;
> this framework is only a starting point when nothing more specific exists.

## Workflow (four stages)

| Stage | Content |
|---|---|
| 1. Confirm the criteria | Which programme? Are criteria public? Scoring format? Deliverable audience? |
| 2. Criterion-by-criterion review | Two criteria × five elements |
| 3. Feasibility and budget check | Timeline, budget-to-scope fit, data availability |
| 4. Composite score and two versions | Candid committee version + constructive applicant version |

### Stage 2: two criteria × five elements

| Criterion | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **Intellectual Merit** | Can this research advance knowledge? |
| **Broader Impacts** | Beyond the research itself, what does it offer society, industry or education? |

Both criteria ask the same five questions: how far does it advance the field? how creative, original,
even transformative is it? is the plan coherent, with a clear success criterion? does the team's
record match the ambition? are the resources (data, site, partners, time) sufficient?

**Two reminders while reviewing:**

- **Broader Impacts is the easiest section to write as a platitude.** Press on "how, specifically" — a pleasant general claim does not count.
- **Multi-theory integration must be real.** A genuine integration has clear relationships among the constructs of the two frameworks, jointly supporting one research question. Listing two theory names side by side while keeping them independent is not integration.

---

## The heart of stage 4: two versions

| Version | Register | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Committee version | Candid | Including judgements that affect funding but should not go to the applicant verbatim (e.g. "this overlaps substantially with another application under review") |
| Applicant version | Constructive | The same judgements, in language the applicant can act on: what to strengthen, and how |

> **The two versions must agree.** Only the wording, and what is appropriate to say, differ.
> Never give contradictory evaluations to spare the applicant's feelings.

## Usage

```
I'm on the faculty FRG review panel and need to score this proposal.
Our criteria: novelty 40%, feasibility 30%, team 20%, budget 10%, out of 100.
Write the review — one version for the committee, one for the applicant.
```

The skill confirms that it will use the faculty criteria (**not** the NSF default), scores each
dimension with reasons for deductions, then produces the two versions.

---

## Boundaries

- **The default framework is not the answer.** When the funder has criteria, they govern.
- **Two versions, one judgement.**
- **Do not let a worthy aim excuse feasibility problems.** However strong the intellectual merit, if feasibility or budget cannot carry it, say so.
- **Specific reasons, not impressions.** Whatever the score, the opinion must let the applicant or the committee see why.
- **It does not decide funding.** It provides scores and reasons; the committee decides.
